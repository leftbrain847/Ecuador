Unresolved · flights decide the shape

Eleven days on the ground assumes a morning arrival and an evening departure. We don't have either yet. Day 1 now runs all the way to Sigchos, which needs us in Latacunga by mid-afternoon — a late landing kills it and we fall back to a Latacunga night.

Book flights first, then cut in this order: third Baños day, then the Chimborazo re-roll day, then the Loop rest day. Protect the rest day if we can — it does acclimatisation work nothing else does.

1 Land Quito, bags in Latacunga, on to Sigchos Sleep Sigchos · 2,850 mTransit 5–6h
Bus

Airport to Quitumbe on the COSIBO city bus, $2, 60–90 minutes. Quitumbe to Latacunga, roughly 2 hours. Drop the non-trek bags at the Latacunga hostel — arrange this in advance, we're not staying — then straight onto a Sigchos bus, another 1.5–2 hours.

Waking up in Sigchos means Day 2 starts on foot at 08:00 instead of after a two-hour bus. It's the single biggest improvement available to the front half.

Gate: we need to be at Latacunga terminal by roughly 15:00 to make this work. If the flight lands after about 10:00, sleep in Latacunga instead and take the 06:00 or 08:00 bus to Sigchos on Day 2. If we clear customs after 18:00, sleep in Quito — the airport bus stops around 22:00.

2 Sigchos → Isinliví Sleep Isinliví · 2,950 mWalk 4–5h · 10 km
On foot

The gentlest day of the Loop: a long descent to the Toachi river, then a steep climb to Isinliví. About 440 m of ascent. Signage runs clearer in this direction than the reverse.

Net altitude gain for the day is tiny — 100 m between the two villages — which is exactly what a first day should look like.

3 Isinliví → Chugchilán Sleep Chugchilán · 3,200 mWalk 5–7h · 12 km
On foot

Down to the valley floor, along the river, then a steep canyon wall up to a viewpoint around 3,300 m. Roughly 790 m of ascent — the biggest climbing day until Quilotoa.

First night above 3,000 m for both of us. Compare notes at dinner: headache, appetite, sleep quality.

4 Rest day Sleep Chugchilán · 3,200 mNothing

Doing nothing is the point. It also does real work as acclimatisation for the higher nights ahead — this is the day that makes Day 7 reasonable rather than reckless.

Morning resting readings from both of us. Baseline for the rest of the trip.

5 Chugchilán → Quilotoa Sleep Crater rim · 3,900 mWalk 5–7h · 13 km
On foot

The hardest day: about 975 m of ascent finishing with a sustained climb to the rim at 3,914 m. Arrive early afternoon for sunset. Sunrise the next morning is the reason this night is in the plan.

Highest sleep of the trip and a 700 m jump from Chugchilán. Confirm heating or enough blankets when we book — it is genuinely cold up there.

6 Sunrise on the rim, then south to Riobamba Sleep Riobamba · 2,750 mBus ~4h total
Bus

Quilotoa → Latacunga runs frequently, about 2–2.5 hours. Then Latacunga → Riobamba, roughly 1.5–2 hours. Collect the stored bags on the way through.

Evening tasks, both of us: check the Chimborazo forecast, confirm the operator pickup time, take resting readings, and agree out loud whether tomorrow is on.

We drop 1,150 m tonight. That's normal and it doesn't undo the acclimatisation — going high for the day and sleeping low is good practice. The Day 7 risk is hours of exertion at 5,000 m, not tonight's altitude. See the Stay tab if we want to sleep at 4,000 m instead.

11 Baños → Quito, fly home Transit 5–6h
Bus

Baños → Quitumbe is about 3.5–4 hours, then Quitumbe → airport another 60–90 minutes, then three hours for an international check-in. That is a nine-hour chain. Tababela sits well east of the city and the transfer is longer than people expect.

Check before every travel day

A state of emergency has been in force across the provinces this route crosses — Pichincha, Cotopaxi, Chimborazo and Tungurahua — with checkpoints and roadblocks that can reroute intercity buses at short notice. The 2025 national strike closed highland roads in Chimborazo and Cotopaxi for weeks at a time; the whole itinerary is one-way and bus-dependent with no alternate.

Live road status: ecu911.gob.ec/consulta-de-vias — bookmark it and look the night before we move.

The bike road

The beginner descent runs 8 km of dirt then about 29 km of asphalt down to San Juan, on the Calpi–San Juan–El Arenal highway. That road has been in poor condition for years — local reporting counted more than 200 potholes on the Calpi–San Juan stretch — and resurfacing was announced in May 2026 with partial one- and two-lane closures between 08:00 and 17:00. The transport ministry posted further asphalt repairs on the same stretch in July 2026.

Twenty-nine kilometres of descent sharing a coned-off lane with interprovincial buses is a different activity from the one the tour descriptions sell. Ask the operator what the paved section looks like right now, and take the answer seriously — the shorter dirt-only option with a van pickup is a fine outcome.

Ten nights

Book now means September fills and there is no second option in these villages. Hold means don't book until the flex block resolves.

Night 1Sigchos · 2,850 mBook now

New — replaces the Latacunga night. Small town, limited beds, so book rather than turn up. Confirm they'll hold a late arrival, since we're coming straight off two buses.

Fallback: a Latacunga bed if the flight lands late. Keep that reservation refundable rather than skipping it entirely.

Night 2Isinliví — Casa Pájaro AzulBook now

Family-run guesthouse in Isinliví — closer to what we actually want out of the Loop than a lodge. Confirm whether dinner and breakfast are included; most Isinliví places do half board because there's nowhere else to eat.

Alternates in the same village: Llullu Llama Mountain Lodge (bigger, spa, the default choice) and Hostal Taita Cristobal (simpler, cheaper).

Nights 3–4Chugchilán · 3,200 mBook now

Two consecutive nights, including the rest day. The Black Sheep Inn sits just outside the village with woodstoves, sauna and hot tub, and rates that include meals — but it's the least family-run option on the route.

More in keeping with the rest: Hostal Cloud Forest and Hostal El Vaquero, both in the village, both cheaper. Worth a look before defaulting to the Inn.

Night 5Quilotoa village · 3,900 mBook now

Fewer rooms, higher prices and genuinely cold. Hostal Chukirawa sits in front of the mirador and has heaters in the rooms. Confirm heating or blanket count in writing.

Alternates: Runa Wasi Quilotoa, Martita's House, Cabañas Quilotoa.

Nights 6–7Riobamba · 2,750 mBook now

Two nights either side of the Chimborazo day. Pick somewhere the operator will collect from at 08:00 and give them the address when we book the tour.

Higher option for night 6: Chimborazo Lodge (Estrella del Chimborazo) or Casa Cóndor at Pulingui, both around 4,000 m inside the reserve, heated, half board. Better acclimatisation and a better night — but no town, no re-roll base, and the operator has to collect from out there. Trade flexibility for altitude only if Chimborazo is the thing we most want.

How the day runs

Composite of what Riobamba operators publish. Confirm the exact version when we book.

08:00–08:30 · Hotel pickup Van from Riobamba, about an hour to the reserve. Register at the control point on the way in — everyone entering has to.
~10:00 · Carrel refuge, 4,850 m Drive right to the parking area. Toilets and a café here. This is where the bikes and the support vehicle wait all day.
10:30 · Walk to Whymper, 5,030–5,060 m About 1 km on a marked stony path, 45 minutes to an hour. Taxing at that altitude, not technical, no gear beyond warm layers.
Optional · Condor Cocha, ~5,100 m Ten minutes further on the short version, or 1.5–2 hours for the longer loop. Decide this at Whymper based on how we're actually doing, not at breakfast.
~13:00 · Back at Carrel, gear up Helmet, elbow and knee pads, safety briefing and a test ride on the flat before anyone commits to the descent.
13:30 · Descend to San Juan 8 km dirt switchbacks then paved road, 4,850 m down to roughly 2,900–3,200 m. No pedalling required. Support vehicle stays with the group.
~14:30 · Lunch in San Juan, van back Back in Riobamba around 15:00. Bikes go home in the van; nothing for us to return.

Go / no-go

Run this twice: the evening of Day 6, and again at the Carrel refuge before we start walking. Either of us can call it off for both of us, no negotiation.

Headache that doesn't clear with water and rest Don't go up. This is the one symptom that reliably precedes the bad version. Stand down
Nausea, dizziness, or a bad night's sleep with breathlessness Stand down and re-roll tomorrow. Riobamba is a fine place to lose a day. Stand down
Symptoms appear on the walk above Carrel Turn around immediately and descend to the van. Descent is the treatment; there is no shelter to wait it out in up there. Turn around
Both of us clear, weather clear Walk to Whymper. Reassess there before adding Condor Cocha — the extension is optional and the mountain isn't going anywhere. Go
One of us clear, one not The one who's fine can still do the day; the one who isn't stays low. Don't split by pushing someone up the hill. Split

Neither of us has been at 5,000 m before. Day 7 gains roughly 2,350 m from where we sleep, in one morning, then adds a long descent in windchill. The Loop nights and the rest day are what make that reasonable — they don't make symptoms negotiable.

Ask the operators

Contact two or three, compare the answers, then book. Julio Verne Travel runs the classic Riobamba hike-and-bike with a beginner and an advanced route; Chimborazo Tours Ecuador and Ecuador Adventures Trip both publish day tours around $65 per person with a two-person minimum.

01Which bike route are we on — the dirt-and-paved beginner route, or the technical off-road one? Confirm in writing that it's the former.
02What condition is the paved section to San Juan in right now, given the resurfacing works? Is there a shorter dirt-only option with a van pickup?
03Support vehicle behind the group the whole way, or meeting us at points?
04Helmet, elbow and knee pads included? Gloves? Any of it optional?
05Guide speaks English? Has the guide turned a group around for altitude before, and what's their threshold?
06If one of us stops at Whymper and one continues, how does that work with a single guide?
07Is the Carrel road open and normal this week? Any reserve restrictions?
08If we cancel the morning of for weather or altitude, what happens to the money and can we re-book for the next day?
09Do you collect from Baños, or from Chimborazo Lodge / Casa Cóndor, and what does that cost?

Options by effort

Everything below the summit is walking. No technical gear anywhere on this list except the last line.

4,850 m Drive to Carrel refuge No walking beyond the parking area. Paved road, no 4x4 needed. Toilets and a café.
5,050 m Carrel → Whymper refuge About an hour, roughly 200 m of gain on a marked path. Taxing at that altitude, not technical.
~5,100 m On to Condor Cocha lagoon Ten minutes beyond Whymper on the short version, or 1.5–2 hours for the longer one.
~5,250 m Whymper → Chamonix Needles Around 2 hours beyond Whymper. Scree and rock, no scrambling. Operators want helmet and poles, and good acclimatisation. Guided · Day 8 only
Full day Needles + descent to La Chorrera The above, then 3–4 hours descending to about 3,900 m and a pickup. The best walking day on this mountain. Guided
37 km Downhill bike — beginner route About 8 km dirt then 29 km paved, from 4,850 m down to San Juan. Support vehicle follows, safety briefing and test ride first. Fits confident riders who aren't mountain bikers. Guided tour · around $65–70 pp
34 km Downhill bike — advanced route Roughly 20 km off road. Singletrack, sand, gravel, tight turns, some uphill. Experienced mountain bikers only. Not us
6,263 m Summit Two days, glacier route, crampons and ice axe, overnight start. Operators want a week of Andean acclimatisation first. Not compatible with this trip. Guide mandatory

Costs & access

Confirm all three with a Riobamba operator before Day 7.

Reserve entry Older operator pages quote about $6 for foreigners and $2 for Ecuadorians. Ecuadorian reporting in 2025 said reserve access is officially free for nationals and foreigners alike. Small money either way — carry small bills and don't be surprised by either answer.
Whymper refuge Reported closed and derelict as accommodation, with water and infrastructure problems, though tours still stop there. Treat it as a waypoint with a toilet, not shelter and not a bail-out point if the weather turns.
Getting up there independently Taxi from Riobamba to the parking area around $35 round trip, or hourly Guaranda-bound buses will drop you at the signed turnoff. Fine for the walk; the bike day needs an operator.
